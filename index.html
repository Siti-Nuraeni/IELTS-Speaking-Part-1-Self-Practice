How Part 1 works

Part 1 lasts 4–5 minutes. The examiner checks your identity, then asks about familiar topics: first about your work, studies, hometown or home, then two other everyday topics. You don’t get preparation time, and the examiner won’t comment on your answers.

The answer formula

Aim for 2–4 sentences (about 20–30 seconds). Too short sounds limited; too long sounds rehearsed.

AnswerReply directly in the first sentence. No “That’s a good question”.
ReasonSay why, using a linker: because, since, mainly for…
DetailAdd an example, a contrast, or a past/future comparison.

Do you prefer printed books or e-books?

I’m a bit old-fashioned in that respect — I prefer printed books.Answer I like the feel of turning the pages, and staring at a screen all day already strains my eyes.Reason Still, e-books are handy when I’m travelling, since I can carry dozens at once.Detail

The band descriptors used for grading

Examiners score four criteria equally. Your answers in this book are graded against these official descriptors. Each criterion gets a whole band, and the overall score is their average, rounded to the nearest half band.

Show bands 3 to 0

Notes from the descriptors: a candidate must fully fit the positive features of the descriptor at a particular level, and the real test rates average performance across all three parts. A Part 1 grade here is an estimate of your level, not an official score.

Do

  • Use the highlighted phrases in the model answers, but only the ones that fit you.
  • Mix tenses: present for habits, past for comparisons, “would / I’d like to” for the future.
  • Buy time naturally: “Hmm, I’d say…”, “To be honest…”, “That’s something I’ve never really thought about, but…”.
  • Be honest. You can change details, but personal answers sound more natural.

Avoid

  • One-word answers like “Yes” or “Not really” with nothing after them.
  • Memorised speeches. Examiners notice, and it can lower your fluency score.
  • Repeating the question word for word at the start of every answer.
  • Forcing idioms that don’t fit (“It’s raining cats and dogs” is a classic giveaway).
